Thanks for running it. The arithmetic is right, and it's actually slightly conservative: FIDE is at [about 2,000 GMs total](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grandmaster_(chess)), [44 of them women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_female_chess_grandmasters), so the male ratio is nearer 128:1 than 135:1 and the gap is 5.4x, not 5.1x. At the male rate you'd expect 237 women holding the title. There are 44. That's about 12 standard deviations out, and for a fixed bar the expected share above the line doesn't depend on how many people you count, so pool size doesn't rescue it either. I'm not going to wave it away.

Where it falls apart is the sentence doing the work: "if those ratios are the same, then you can logically conclude that men and women are roughly the same at the game."

That inference doesn't hold in either direction. A count above a fixed threshold isn't a measure of central ability. It's a function of the whole shape of the distribution and of how selectively people enter the pool at all. Two groups with identical ability can produce very different rates above 2500 if they're drawn into rated chess on different terms. Two groups with different ability can produce identical rates. The test can't separate the hypothesis you're testing from its alternative. That isn't a quibble about the data, it's the design.

You can also see it has no power because your number is already in the piece. Headcount explains about half the 275 point gap, leaving roughly 139 Elo unaccounted for. Invert your ratio: a 5.4x difference in the fraction clearing 2500 implies a distribution shift of 133 to 164 Elo, depending on what you assume for the spread of the pool. That brackets 139. Tail counts are violently sensitive to small shifts in the middle, which is why the two numbers look unrelated while being the same finding.

One point in your favour before the part where I went and checked. The obvious objection to your ratio is that you're dividing a lifetime title stock accumulated since 1950 by a snapshot of who's registered now, over a period when women mostly couldn't compete on the same terms. [FIDE's president had to rule in 1977](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_chess) that women couldn't be barred from a national championship. Susan Polgar was the first to earn the title through the ordinary norm route, in 1991. 86 percent of all female GM titles have been awarded since 2000. That objection is methodologically correct and it does not rescue the number: run it as a flow instead, 12 women titled in the last 11 years, and per active female player that's still about a fifth of the male rate. Roughly 5x again. The head start is real, it just isn't the explanation.

So your comment sent me back to the data to ask the question properly, which is whether that 5.4x is about strength or about what happens to women between a rating and a title.

I took the 130 monthly FIDE rating lists behind the original piece, July 2015 to April 2026, kept each player's peak rating, and asked what fraction holds a norm title (IM or GM) conditional on peak rating. Per the [title regulations](https://handbook.fide.com/chapter/B012024) those need norms across at least 27 games in international events, so they're the ones that depend on invitations, travel budget and which circuit you play. If women are weaker, the rates should match at fixed rating, because a 2450 is a 2450. If the bottleneck is access, they shouldn't.

They match.

- Peak 2400 to 2500: 84.0 percent of women hold IM or GM (100 of 119), against 81.0 percent of men (2,569 of 3,171). Fisher p = 0.47.
- Peak 2500 to 2600: 100 percent of women (29 of 29), 98.7 percent of men.

At 2300 to 2400 women are behind, 13.5 against 20.8 percent, but that's the one band where the parallel women's ladder confounds it, since a 2350 WGM isn't necessarily chasing an IM norm. Above that, where the ladder tops out, the gap is gone. I'd also meant to use FM and CM as a rating-only control, and that doesn't work: FIDE writes women's titles into the same field, so a woman who'd qualify for FM shows up as WGM instead. Worth knowing if you go looking yourself.

So the title machinery is not where women are lost. Conditional on getting to 2400, they convert at the same rate you do. What actually moves is who arrives. Women are 10.4 percent of all rated players, but only 1,840 of the 39,783 players who ever reached 2150, which is 4.6 percent, and it keeps falling: 4.4 percent of the 2300 to 2400 band, 3.6 percent at 2400 to 2500, 2.7 percent at 2500 to 2600, 0.88 percent above 2600. Half the attrition has already happened before anyone is near a norm.

Which puts the whole thing back on the 139 Elo, and that is where the variation among women is the argument. [Chabris and Glickman](https://glicko.net/research/ps-final.pdf) found the US junior gap ran 110 to 200 points overall, but pooling the areas where girls were at least half the entrants it fell to 35.2 points and lost significance (p = .59), and in Oakland, at 68 percent girls, the girls averaged higher. One environmental variable moves the rating gap by about as much as the entire residual we're trying to explain, with girls on both sides of it. Between federations it's the same shape: Mongolia, Sri Lanka and Uganda top [FIDE's own Gender Equality in Chess Index](http://wom.fide.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Report_2023_FIDE_GECI.pdf), the Netherlands sits 100th and Denmark 105th of 105, and Georgia alone holds 5 of the 44 female GM titles.

So your statistic is real, and it isn't about conversion, because conversion is equal. It's about a rating distribution whose position moves with who's in the room. That's a claim about circumstances, and it's testable, which is more than the ability reading has managed.
