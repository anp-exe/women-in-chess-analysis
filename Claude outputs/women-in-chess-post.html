I spent the last few months pulling apart eleven years of FIDE rating data to answer one question: why are there so few women at the top of chess?

In 2009 Merim Bilalić and his colleagues asked how much of the Elo gap in chess was down to participation. Their answer: 96% of it, with the headline conclusion:

"There is little left for biological and cultural explanations to account for."

That is a large percentage. It is also one national federation, in Germany.

So, how much of it holds at the FIDE elite?

I explored this and a few other burning questions on almost eleven years of FIDE rating data, 130 monthly snapshots from July 2015 to April 2026, plus around 12,000 chess.com profiles pulled from the public API.

1. If women played in the same numbers, how big would the gap be?

At the FIDE top 100, the gap between men and women is 275 Elo. The question is how much of that is there simply because men have far more players to produce an exceptional one from.

Picture every chess rating there is, from absolute beginner up to Magnus Carlsen, written on tickets and tipped into one drum. One drum, so there is no such thing as a men's ticket or a women's ticket.

Now pull out 251,137 tickets and call that pile the men, then pull out 30,420 and call that pile the women, matching the active men and women in FIDE's April 2026 list, counted on their 12-month average ratings. Compare the best 100 in each pile. I did that 6,000 times.

Same drum every time, and the men's pile still comes out 136 Elo ahead at the top 100. That is exactly half of the 275 point gap FIDE actually shows, and it happened with nothing separating the two groups except how many tickets each one got. Go deeper into the list and that share climbs: 61 percent at the top 1,000, 68 percent across the top 10,000.

The more people who play, the more chances at an exceptional player.

2. Do women in the FIDE elite peak earlier than men?

There is a long-standing idea that girls develop early and then stall, while boys keep improving into their thirties.

No. Among the top 25 active players a side, women peak at 29.2 and men at 28.7. At the top 100 it flips the other way, 28.3 against 29.7. Both differences are small enough to be noise (p = 0.84 for the top 25), which is the statistical way of saying there is nothing here.

The idea that women peak young and fade is not in this data.

3. Did The Queen's Gambit boost female participation in FIDE?

When Netflix released The Queen's Gambit in October 2020, the chess world expected a revolution. A fictional orphan named Beth Harmon beat every male grandmaster in sight, millions of viewers discovered the game, and the streaming numbers broke records.

If a television programme was ever going to change who plays chess, it was that one. So did it?

Well. It is inconclusive, and mostly for a boring reason.

Covid killed over-the-board tournaments, and you need tournament play to get a FIDE rating, so everything that happened in late 2020 happened on chess.com instead.

Men's and women's registrations fall and recover together. That is what a pandemic looks like, not a female-specific surge.

Chess.com is a different picture.

To measure the boom I first worked out what the site's growth would have looked like if it had simply carried on as before, with no show, no lockdown and no streaming surge. Then I compared that to what actually happened.

The gap between the two is about 84 million extra signups by December 2024, and four years on the curve still has not come back down to where the old trend said it should be.

It cannot all be the show, though. Lockdown, the streaming boom and Beth Harmon all landed in the same eighteen months, and nothing in this data can separate them.

So: nothing in FIDE, an enormous amount on chess.com, and no way to prove which part of late 2020 did it.

4. When will women make up 50% of FIDE players?

If more players is what produces more elite players, the obvious question is how quickly women's numbers are growing.

Disclaimer: this is not a forecast. It just shows what would happen if the growth of the last decade carried on at exactly the same pace.

The answer? 2479.🥲 Women went from 9.49 percent of active rated players in July 2015 to 10.43 percent in April 2026, or more than eight men for every woman. At that pace none of us will live to see parity.

5. What is the other half?

Half the gap was headcount. The other 139 Elo is not, and my data cannot tell you where it comes from. The published research can narrow it down.

  • Early environment. Where girls make up at least half of the new young players in an area, their starting ratings are no lower than boys'. More girls playing, smaller gap, right from the first rated game. (Chabris & Glickman 2006; Li, Glickman & Chabris 2025)
  • Tournament culture. The 2023 French open letter, written by 14 players and now carrying more than 120 signatories, names harassment and assault among the main reasons women and teenage girls stop playing.

Both of those are claims about circumstances rather than capacity.

6. So where does that leave us?

The conclusion is not that women are worse at chess. It is that we are producing far fewer elite women than we should be, and that closing the headcount gap on its own would not finish the job. The sample size half is the easy half. The cultural half takes longer.

The headcount half has obvious levers. More girls starting, and starting somewhere they are not the only one. The clearest finding in the research is about critical mass: where girls make up at least half of the new young players, their starting ratings match the boys'. That is an argument for girls' sections, women's events and clubs built to be somebody's first room rather than their fiftieth.

There is a catch worth knowing about. Maass and colleagues found women played worse when they believed their opponent was a man, and Rothgerber and Wolsiefer found the same in players aged five to fifteen. Then Stafford went through 6.6 million games from 461,637 rated players and found no such effect at all, and suggested that by the time you are a rated tournament player you are too professionalised for it to bite.

Which is roughly what my own numbers say. Nothing looks wrong at the top: women there peak at the same age as men. The losses happen earlier, before anyone has a rating worth analysing, and that is exactly where women-only spaces are aimed.
